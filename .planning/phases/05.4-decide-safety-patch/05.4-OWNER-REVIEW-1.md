Monthly surplus is not the same as spendable money
The cases frequently calculate affordability using:
\[
\text{Monthly income}-\text{Bills}-\text{Spending}
\]

That tells you what remains over a typical month. It does not tell you whether the money is available today.

For example, Dev has $1,400 on November 15. The app says $1,000 remains for November before card payments.

But we do not know whether the $400 in everyday spending happens before or after November 25.

The app needs to account for:
- The current balance.
- What bills are already paid.
- What spending remains.
- The dates of required payments.
- When income actually arrives.
- The lowest projected account balance before the next income.
Recommendation: Every verdict should be based on a date-ordered cash-flow projection, not just monthly arithmetic.
B. The app should distinguish three separate decisions
Can pay

The person has sufficient available funds to complete the transaction right now.
Fits the cash-flow plan

The transaction does not cause an upcoming bill or minimum payment to go unpaid.
Financially safe

The purchase also preserves an appropriate reserve and does not create unacceptable debt or liquidity risk.
A purchase could pass the first two tests and fail the third.

Currently, your cases often use Fits as though passing the cash-flow test automatically establishes financial safety.
C. Existing debt is being treated too passively
DB-04 and DB-09 are particularly revealing.

Sam has £6,000 of card debt at 24.9%. Yet a £1,800 holiday is considered affordable because £2,000 is technically available.

This may be mathematically correct under a narrow cash-flow definition, but it is financially incomplete.

The app should not necessarily prohibit discretionary spending because someone has debt. However, it should distinguish:
- Cash affordability.
- Debt servicing.
- Cost of carrying balances.
- Implications for savings and reserves.
- Whether the purchase introduces more high-interest borrowing.
Debt information can remain advisory without automatically changing the cash-flow verdict, but it should not disappear from the overall assessment of financial risk.

2

Load future dated cash flows

3

Reconcile obligations

4

5

Calculate daily projected balances

Identify shortfalls and their dates

Check available reserves

7

Evaluate financing risk

Rates, total costs, existing debt and repayment exposure

Recommended mathematical checks
For a one-time purchase, at a minimum:
\[
B_t=B_0+\sum_{i\le t}I_i-\sum_{i\le t}E_i-P_t
\]

Where:
- \(B_t\) is the projected available cash at date \(t\).

7. Recommended calculation architecture
I would build the engine around four independent layers.

Layer 1 — Actual liquidity

Determine how much is currently spendable, including account balances, pending debits, accessible savings, transfer restrictions and any committed funds.

Simulate dated income, bills, expected spending and transfers. Evaluate the lowest available balance over the relevant period, not just the end-of-month total.

Apply configured safety-net protection, minimum balances, scheduled contributions, savings targets and emergency-spending exceptions.

3. Six structural weaknesses in the affordability model
Weakness 1 — Treating overdraft as spending power
An overdraft is borrowed money, not disposable income.

Your current approach recognizes overdraft availability, but it does not consistently evaluate whether using it is financially sustainable.

A purchase might be technically possible while still being a poor financial decision.

Fix: Separate these three questions:
- Can the bank account fund the transaction?
- Can the person repay any borrowing created?
- Can they afford the purchase without undermining future obligations?
Weakness 2 — Using monthly aggregates instead of dated cash flows
Monthly surplus does not establish when money is available.

Consider someone with $1,000 projected monthly surplus, $200 in their account, an $800 bill tomorrow, and $2,000 salary arriving next week.

A $100 purchase appears affordable under a monthly budget but creates an immediate problem.

Fix: Simulate every known transaction in date order and calculate the minimum projected available balance.
Weakness 3 — No clear treatment of uncertain data
Tom's case exposes this weakness.

A missing transaction, unreliable income forecast, unknown overdraft limit, or unsynchronized account should reduce confidence in an affordability decision.

Fix: Add an explicit confidence or data-quality state. For example, the engine can report "Not yet — records incomplete" without pretending to know the exact future available balance.
Weakness 4 — Overdraft interest modeled incorrectly
The document uses the maximum overdraft balance multiplied by the number of days until payday.

That can misstate the expected cost.

A more accurate daily-interest estimate is:
\[
I=\sum_{d=1}^{n}\max(0,-B_d)\times\frac{r}{365}
\]

where \(B_d\) is each day's closing balance and \(r\) is the annual interest rate, assuming a 365-day simple-interest convention.

For purchase-specific costs, simulate with and without the proposed purchase and compare the interest and fees generated by each path.
Weakness 5 — Account aggregation obscures funding restrictions
OD-08 illustrates this problem.

Money in one account may be reserved, restricted, jointly held, or unavailable for another account's payments.

Fix: Track account-specific balances and permitted transfers. Aggregate wealth is useful for a dashboard, but it is not automatically spendable cash.
Weakness 6 — Future affordability dates are overconfident
The scenarios sometimes identify payday or a future month as the first affordable moment without fully simulating that period.

Fix: A future date should only be returned when the transaction can be funded on that date and the subsequent financial path remains within the defined limits.
